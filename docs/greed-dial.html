The Greed Dial

The economy as stocks, flows, and feedback loops — Machinations-style — plus three toy simulations you can push around. Nothing here is the real game; every curve is a caricature built from the real constants, which is exactly what makes the loops visible.

map

One economy, two loops

Solid arrows move resources. Dashed arrows are state — a stock's level changing a rate somewhere else. The game is the fight between one reinforcing loop and one balancing loop, and the feed slider decides which one wins.

pool (stock) source / drain converter resource flow state connection
R1 · REINFORCING
The village grows. Drops → houses → workers → faster chopping → more drops. Left alone, production compounds. Every builder game has this loop; it is why the mid-game snowballs.
B1 · BALANCING
The night answers. Drops → fed to the thing → XP tier → bigger waves → dead workers, chipped base → less production. This is the greed dial: the player chooses how hard the balancing loop bites, which is rare — most games author it.
exp·01

Feed it or fight it

One tick = a full day-night cycle. Each day the village produces; you split the haul between feeding (XP now, bigger nights later), towers, and houses. Waves scale with the XP tier reached at dusk — 12 + 3·tier spawns against your kill capacity. Find the dial position where the run thrives; then find the two ways it dies.

Grounded in authored data: XP tiers [40,100,200,350], wave 12 + 3·tier spawns, tower cost 16, house cost 14 + 7·houses (HOUSE_COST_ESCALATION), 4 workers per house. Caricatures: a worker gathers ~12 units/day (75 s day ÷ 2.35 s per chop, heavily taxed by walking), a tower kills ~5 spawns/night, a worker ~1. Those two kill numbers are pure guesses — the first thing a headless sim of the real simulation.js would replace.

exp·02

Chop against carry

The open question from the ground-sourcing experiment: is production or logistics the bottleneck? Supply is chopping; capacity is couriers walking drops to the site. When the orange bar beats the blue one, the pile on the ground grows without bound — and hauling distance, not worker count, is usually the culprit.

Real constants: chop every 2.35 s (WORKER_HIT_COOLDOWN), walk 52 px/s (WORKER_SPEED), carry 3 (WORKER_CARRY), ~4.5 s pickup/deposit overhead per trip (guess). Courier throughput = 3 ÷ (2·distance/52 + 4.5). The break-even distance falls out of the sliders.

exp·03

The handoff — when automation passes your hand

The design promise is “you start doing everything yourself; you end deciding everything.” This sim maps the transition points on that road. Your hand improves linearly (upgrades, bought rarely); the village compounds (houses fund houses). Every run of this chart has the same shape — a slow blue line and a red curve that eventually leaves it behind. The design question is not if but which day, and what you were buying along the way. Investment beyond the hand is auto-spent on whichever is the better marginal deal that day: a new house, or a worker-wide upgrade.

Model: your hand starts at 20 units/day and each hand upgrade multiplies it ×1.4 (cost 12·2ᵏ — escalating like the obelisk list should). A worker gathers 12/day; a house (14 + 7·n) adds two; a worker-wide upgrade (“worker fuel”, 17·2ʲ) multiplies every worker ×1.25. The auto-buyer compares rate-gained-per-unit-spent and takes the better deal — watch the event log flip from houses to fuel as the village grows: that flip is itself a transition point worth authoring toward.
